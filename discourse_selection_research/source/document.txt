An Economic Model of Discourse Participation

*Why can a position appear more prevalent than it actually is?*

*Why can a debate persist even when nobody becomes more extreme?*

*Why might today's participants reflect yesterday's incentives?*

Model

When the benefits and costs of speaking covary with beliefs, differential participation, persistence and amplification can distort apparent prevalence. Experts answering questions can also become overrepresented; selection need not reduce quality.

If satisfied participants leave sooner than motivated disputants, debate can persist without anyone becoming more extreme. This explanation requires exit to depend on beliefs or motivation, not merely that a debate continues.

Past approval, rejection and expression costs may change future participation. Fixed differences in entry and retention already generate selection; feedback that changes these probabilities makes participation depend on incentive history.

To model return decisions, use a daily, myopic random-utility baseline. For account i on day t, Aᵢ(t) is 1 if it comments in the thread and 0 otherwise. Time t counts days since the paper's release. Let α be baseline net benefit, δ its daily decline, and ρ the association with previous-day participation. A person participates when this net benefit plus a standard logistic random shock is positive. Writing σ(z)=1/(1+exp(−z)), the participation probability conditional on observed history is:

Pr[Aᵢ(t)=1] = σ[α − δt + ρAᵢ(t−1)].

Let q(t) be the probability of returning after an inactive day, and s(t) the probability of continuing after an active day. Thus q(t)=σ(α−δt) and s(t)=σ(α−δt+ρ). Positive ρ can reflect reinforcement, habit or persistent differences between people; it does not identify causal feedback.

Belief entrenchment is an optional extension. Let eᵢ(t) denote resistance to revising a private belief. Public commitments and feedback may change e, affecting subsequent participation and updating after counterevidence. These private states are unobserved here; the dashed paths in Figure 1 are not fitted.

Data

The case is the LessWrong release thread for Alignment Faking in Large Language Models, December 18, 2024–January 1, 2025 UTC. A November 19, 2026 snapshot contains 53 comments from 27 accounts. Starting the day after each account's first contribution, there are 352 account-days at risk and seven active account-days. Each account-day records whether that account comments at least once; multiple same-day comments count once. I condition on first-contribution dates and fit subsequent decisions by maximum likelihood. Initial arrivals are inputs, not predictions; deleted historical comments cannot be recovered.

Results

Compare three specifications: constant participation probability; declining net benefit; and declining benefit plus previous-day activity. Their AIC scores, which penalize model complexity, are 70.7, 40.0 and 41.9 respectively. Lower is better. The two-parameter decline model is preferred, with α=1.46 and δ=1.67. Its expected returning-account counts on December 19–21 are 3.14, 2.94 and 0.74, versus 3, 3 and 1 observed.

Leaving out one account at a time also favors decline over the constant model: held-out negative log likelihood falls from 35.53 to 20.67. This tests other accounts' subsequent decisions, not future dates. Adding recent activity yields ρ=0.45, but improves fit little and is unstable in some account holdouts. The fit describes a short burst of thread participation. It does not establish belief-dependent selection, selective exit or responses to measured rewards; with seven return events and no private-belief measurements, entrenchment remains a hypothesis.
